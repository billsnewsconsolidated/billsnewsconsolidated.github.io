No. 1 offense meets No. 1 defense Monday night. History says neither survives intact.

Buffalo at Minnesota on Monday night is just the seventh time since 2017 that the No. 1 offense in EPA per play meets the No. 1 defense, per Buffalo Rumblings. History's verdict: a 3-3 split, the top offense's EPA collapsing from +0.217 to +0.059 — and somebody's ranking exposed.

By MATT MANZELLA · Film Room · Nov 4, 2026

Monday night in Minneapolis is not just 6-1 against 6-1. It is the rarest kind of regular-season collision the modern NFL produces. When Buffalo visits the Minnesota Vikings on Monday, Nov. 9 at 8:15 PM ET at U.S. Bank Stadium, it will be just the seventh time since 2017 that the No. 1 offense in EPA per play meets the No. 1 defense in EPA allowed per play, per Buffalo Rumblings' breakdown published Wednesday. EPA per play is not a vanity ranking — Rumblings notes it is a truer measure of a unit's quality than yards or points alone. So strip away the records, the dome and the prime-time lights, and Monday is simple: the best offense in football, against the best defense in football, with one of them walking out looking ordinary.

History says 'ordinary' is exactly what happens — to somebody. The previous six No. 1-versus-No. 1 meetings split evenly, 3-3, per Rumblings: the Ravens beat the Patriots in 2019, the Buccaneers humbled Green Bay in 2020, Buffalo itself beat Kansas City in 2021, Tampa Bay beat Buffalo in overtime later that season, the Chiefs rolled the 49ers in 2022, and the Texans edged the Colts last season. Stretch the sample back to 2001 and the No. 1 offense holds a modest 10-6 edge — but the cost of the ticket shows up in the numbers. The No. 1 offense in those games averaged just 25.6 points, its EPA per play collapsed from +0.217 entering the game to +0.059 during it, and in seven of the 16 matchups the best offense in football actually posted a negative EPA per play. Nobody stays No. 1 on both sides of the ball Monday night. Somebody's ranking is a costume, and the game strips it off.

The defense trying to do the stripping belongs to Brian Flores, and it does not bluff so much as it floods. Minnesota has blitzed at a 69.9% rate this season, per TSN's tracking — a figure no team has approached through seven weeks since ESPN Research began tracking pass rushes in 2006, against a league historical average of 28.9%. Against Indianapolis, Flores sent extra rushers on 85.2% of Daniel Jones' dropbacks, the highest single-game blitz rate of the Next Gen Stats era. And yet the twist that should worry Joe Brady most is the opposite one: per the same TSN reporting, Minnesota's defensive EPA has been substantially better when it rushes four or fewer than when it blitzes. The threat of the blitz is the weapon. Flores, who praised Josh Allen's development at length this week, per ClutchPoints, does not need to send six to beat you — he needs you to spend all week preparing for six, then beat you with four and a coverage picture that changed after the snap.

That coverage picture is the second half of the chess match. Per NBC Sports' matchup breakdown, Minnesota plays the fewest man coverage in the NFL at 5.3% and the second-most zone at 86.5% — a defense that keeps its eyes on the quarterback, rallies to the ball and dares Allen to be patient for twelve plays at a time. Allen, per the same breakdown, has completed 69.7% of his passes against zone this season, and he arrives with the season line ClutchPoints cited this week: 135 of 205 for 1,793 yards, 11 touchdowns and five interceptions, fresh off a three-total-touchdown afternoon against Baltimore in which he threw for 289 yards. Patience, though, has never been the question with Allen. The question is whether patience survives the fourth quarter of a one-score game in a dome that is shaking — because that is exactly when No. 17 stops dinking, starts hunting, and gives a zone defense the one thing it is built to steal.

Which is why James Cook might be the most important Bill in Minneapolis. Minnesota's defense finally cracked last week, losing its first game of the season, 19-15 in Detroit — and per Vikings Territory's Week 9 breakdown, the Lions torched the Vikings on the ground in that loss. Cook is the reigning NFL rushing champion, and his 2026 log, per Pro Football Network, reads 138 carries for 707 yards at 5.1 a carry with seven touchdowns through seven games. Every efficient Cook run is a blitz that never gets called, a third-and-manageable instead of a third-and-9 against a defense that lives for obvious passing downs, and a minute of rest for Buffalo's own defense. If Buffalo has to drop back 45 times to win this game, Flores wins the math even if he loses some snaps. If Cook forces Minnesota to defend the run honestly, Allen gets to play against a defense that is guessing — and nobody in football guesses worse for Flores than a quarterback who can also run through the blitz when it finally does come.

The honest caveats belong on the record before kickoff. Minnesota's offense is the weak half of its own building — it has struggled all season behind a defense carrying the record — and quarterback Kyler Murray is questionable with a hamstring injury but expected to play, per NBC Sports. For Buffalo, the week's real information has not arrived yet: a Monday game means the Bills' first practice report does not land until Thursday, and the names to watch on it are wide receivers Joshua Palmer, who injured his hamstring against Baltimore, and DJ Moore, who gave Buffalo a late knee scare Sunday before saying afterward that he was OK, per NFL Network's Cameron Wolfe — plus right tackle Spencer Brown, who missed the Ravens game with a back issue. None of those are designations, and Film Room will not print them as such. They are the difference between Buffalo arriving at full strength for a No. 1-versus-No. 1 fight and arriving with duct tape on the edges.

So here is the verdict history hands us, and it is the only prediction this matchup honestly supports. Buffalo's offense will not look like the No. 1 offense in football for four quarters Monday night — nobody's does in these games; the EPA cliff from +0.217 to +0.059 is the tax the No. 1 defense collects. The game will be won in the margins the rankings do not capture: whether Cook keeps Flores honest, whether Allen takes the zone's underneath money without forcing the deep shot that ends a drive, and whether Jim Leonhard's suddenly aggressive Buffalo defense — the story of last Sunday — can hold a struggling Minnesota offense to the kind of night where 24 points is enough. The No. 1 offense scores 25.6 points on average in these games, per Rumblings. In a dome, in prime time, against this blitz, 25 points and a defense that just found itself might be exactly the formula. One ranking falls Monday night. Film Room is betting it is not Buffalo's.
